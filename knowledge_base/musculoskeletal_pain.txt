MUSCULOSKELETAL PAIN — BACK PAIN, ARTHRITIS, AND JOINT DISORDERS
=================================================================

LOW BACK PAIN (LBP)
=====================

Epidemiology
------------
Low back pain is the leading cause of disability worldwide and the most common reason for absence from work.
Approximately 80% of adults experience LBP at some point in their lifetime.

Classification
--------------
1. Non-specific (mechanical) LBP (90%): no identifiable structural cause
2. Specific LBP (10%): associated with specific pathology (disc herniation, stenosis, spondylolisthesis)
3. Secondary LBP: referred pain from visceral causes (renal, aortic, gynaecological)

Red Flags — Serious Pathology:
- Cauda equina syndrome: bilateral leg pain/weakness, saddle anaesthesia, bowel/bladder dysfunction — EMERGENCY
- Spinal cord compression: progressive neurological deficit
- Cancer (spinal metastases): age >50, history of malignancy, unexplained weight loss, night pain
- Infection (discitis/osteomyelitis): fever, IV drug use, recent spinal procedure
- Fracture: trauma, osteoporosis, long-term steroids
- Inflammatory (ankylosing spondylitis): age <40, morning stiffness >45min, improves with exercise

Clinical Assessment
-------------------
- Site, radiation (leg pain, sciatica — L4/L5/S1), character, severity (VAS/NRS 0-10)
- Duration: acute (<6 weeks), subacute (6-12 weeks), chronic (>12 weeks)
- Aggravating/relieving factors
- Neurological symptoms: weakness, numbness, tingling in limbs
- Bladder/bowel dysfunction (cauda equina)
- Previous episodes, treatments tried

Examination:
- Posture and gait
- Range of motion: flexion, extension, lateral flexion
- Palpation: paravertebral muscle spasm, sacroiliac tenderness
- Straight leg raise (SLR) test: positive if radiating pain <60° — suggests disc herniation/nerve root compression
- Neurological: power, sensation, reflexes in lower limbs (L3, L4, L5, S1 distribution)

Investigations
--------------
- Routine imaging NOT required for non-specific acute LBP
- MRI spine: indicated if red flags, neurological deficit, or considering surgery
- X-ray lumbosacral spine: if fracture, spondylolisthesis, or inflammatory suspected
- Bloods: ESR, CRP, alkaline phosphatase if infection/malignancy suspected; HLA-B27 for inflammatory

Treatment — Non-Specific LBP
-------------------------------
First-line (Evidence-based):
1. Reassurance and patient education: positive messages about recovery
2. Encourage activity: bed rest is HARMFUL; return to normal activities as soon as possible
3. Heat application: effective for acute muscle spasm relief

Analgesia:
- Step 1: Paracetamol 1g QDS (evidence limited but safe first step)
- Step 2: NSAIDs (ibuprofen, naproxen, diclofenac): most effective for LBP
  Use lowest effective dose for shortest time; gastroprotection with PPI
- Step 3: Short-term muscle relaxants (diazepam 2–5mg nocte; methocarbamol)
  Only 1–2 weeks; avoid long-term
- Step 4: Weak opioids (tramadol, codeine/paracetamol combinations): short-term only
- Avoid: strong opioids for non-specific LBP (risk > benefit)

Physiotherapy and Exercise:
- Active exercise programmes (individualised physiotherapy)
- Core strengthening, flexibility, aerobic exercise
- Manual therapy (spinal manipulation): short-term benefit
- McKenzie method for disc-related pain

Psychological:
- Cognitive behavioural therapy (CBT) for chronic LBP with psychosocial "yellow flags"
- Yellow flags (risk factors for chronicity): fear-avoidance beliefs, depression, poor coping,
  job dissatisfaction, legal/compensation issues

Chronic LBP (>12 weeks):
- Multidisciplinary pain management programmes
- Amitriptyline/duloxetine for neuropathic component
- Gabapentin/pregabalin: evidence limited; use with caution
- Epidural steroid injections: for radiculopathy (temporary benefit)
- Surgery: only for structural pathology with neurological compromise

OSTEOARTHRITIS (OA)
====================
- Most common joint disease; degenerative articular cartilage loss
- Commonly affects: knee, hip, hand (DIP joints), spine

Symptoms:
- Pain: use-related, worsens with activity, relieved by rest
- Stiffness: brief morning stiffness (<30 minutes)
- Reduced range of motion, crepitus on movement

Management:
- Core: patient education, weight loss (reduces knee load), physiotherapy, exercise
- Pharmacological: topical NSAIDs (knee/hand), oral NSAIDs, paracetamol, duloxetine
- Intra-articular corticosteroid injections: short-term relief
- Joint replacement (TKR/THR): end-stage OA with failed conservative management

RHEUMATOID ARTHRITIS (RA)
===========================
- Systemic autoimmune inflammatory arthritis
- Features: symmetric small joint polyarthritis, prolonged morning stiffness (>45 min)
- Joints: MCP, PIP joints; wrists, knees, feet
- Extra-articular: rheumatoid nodules, serositis, vasculitis, interstitial lung disease

Investigations:
- RF (rheumatoid factor): 70% positive
- Anti-CCP antibodies: highly specific (95%)
- ESR, CRP (elevated)
- X-rays: erosions, periarticular osteopenia (late)

Treatment:
- DMARDs (Disease-Modifying Antirheumatic Drugs): methotrexate (first-line), hydroxychloroquine,
  sulfasalazine, leflunomide
- Biologics: anti-TNF (etanercept, adalimumab); if inadequate response to DMARDs
- JAK inhibitors: tofacitinib, baricitinib
- Steroids: bridging therapy; intra-articular for acute flares
- Monitor: liver function (methotrexate), infection risk (biologics), FBC
