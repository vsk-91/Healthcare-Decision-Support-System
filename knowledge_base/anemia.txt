IRON DEFICIENCY ANAEMIA
========================

Overview
--------
Iron deficiency anaemia (IDA) is the most common nutritional deficiency worldwide, affecting
approximately 1–2 billion people. It occurs when iron stores are depleted, leading to reduced
haemoglobin synthesis and microcytic hypochromic anaemia.

Causes and Risk Groups
-----------------------
1. Increased iron loss (most common cause in adults):
   - Gastrointestinal bleeding: peptic ulcer, colorectal cancer, IBD, oesophageal varices,
     angiodysplasia, use of NSAIDs/aspirin
   - Menstrual blood loss (heavy periods — most common in premenopausal women)
   - Other: haematuria, pulmonary haemosiderosis, regular blood donation

2. Inadequate intake:
   - Restrictive diets (vegan/vegetarian without supplementation)
   - Poverty, food insecurity
   - Eating disorders

3. Malabsorption:
   - Coeliac disease (most important to exclude in adults)
   - Gastrectomy, bariatric surgery
   - H. pylori infection
   - Inflammatory bowel disease

4. Increased demand:
   - Pregnancy (increased requirement for foetal development)
   - Rapid growth (infants, adolescents)

Clinical Features
------------------
Symptoms (relate to severity and rate of onset):
- Fatigue, lethargy, reduced exercise tolerance
- Pallor (skin, conjunctivae, nail beds, palmar creases)
- Dyspnoea on exertion
- Palpitations, tachycardia
- Headache, poor concentration, irritability
- Cold intolerance

Specific features of iron deficiency (beyond anaemia):
- Angular stomatitis (cracking at corners of mouth)
- Glossitis (smooth, red, painful tongue)
- Koilonychia (spoon-shaped nails)
- Pica (craving non-food items: ice, clay, soil — pagophagia)
- Plummer-Vinson/Paterson-Kelly syndrome (oesophageal web + IDA)
- Restless legs syndrome association

Laboratory Investigations
--------------------------
Full Blood Count:
- Low haemoglobin (Hb): <130 g/L men; <120 g/L women; <110 g/L pregnancy
- Low MCV (microcytosis): <80 fL
- Low MCH (hypochromia)
- Elevated RDW (red cell distribution width) — anisocytosis
- Low reticulocyte count

Iron Studies:
- Serum ferritin: LOW (<12–30 μg/L) — most specific for iron deficiency
  (Note: ferritin is an acute phase reactant — may be falsely normal in inflammation)
- Serum iron: LOW
- TIBC (total iron-binding capacity): HIGH (>70 μmol/L)
- Transferrin saturation: LOW (<20%)

Blood film: microcytic hypochromic red cells, anisocytosis, poikilocytosis

Additional Tests to Identify Cause:
- Upper and lower GI endoscopy (if GI blood loss suspected, especially post-menopausal women and all men)
- Coeliac serology: anti-tTG IgA (screen all adults with IDA)
- H. pylori: stool antigen or urea breath test
- Urine dipstick (haematuria)
- Menstrual history (LMP, heaviness, clots)

Treatment
----------
Oral Iron Therapy:
- First-line: Ferrous sulfate 200mg (65mg elemental iron) three times daily
- Alternatives: ferrous gluconate, ferrous fumarate (better tolerated)
- Take on empty stomach for maximum absorption; if GI side effects: take with food or reduce dose
- Vitamin C (ascorbic acid) enhances non-haem iron absorption
- Avoid tea, coffee, milk within 2 hours (reduce absorption)
- Expected response: reticulocyte rise in 7–10 days; Hb rise 1–2 g/L/week
- Continue therapy for 3 months after Hb normalises to replenish stores

Intravenous Iron:
- Indicated when: oral intolerance, malabsorption, non-compliance, severe anaemia requiring rapid correction,
  pregnancy third trimester, IBD, pre-surgery, renal anaemia on dialysis
- Preparations: ferric carboxymaltose, iron sucrose, ferumoxytol
- Risk: hypersensitivity reactions (resuscitation facilities required)
- Advantages: rapid Hb correction, single infusion, no GI side effects

Blood Transfusion:
- Reserved for: Hb <70 g/L with symptoms, or haemodynamic instability
- Not a substitute for iron therapy

Dietary Advice:
- Haem iron (better absorbed): red meat, liver, poultry, fish
- Non-haem iron (plant-based): lentils, beans, fortified cereals, spinach, dried fruit
- Enhance absorption: consume with vitamin C (citrus juice, tomatoes)
- Reduce inhibitors: avoid tea/coffee/calcium-rich foods at mealtimes

Monitoring:
- Repeat FBC and ferritin after 4 weeks of therapy
- Target: Hb normalisation and ferritin >50 μg/L
- Investigate for underlying cause: GI endoscopy, coeliac testing mandatory in unexplained IDA

When to Refer:
- Unexplained IDA (GI investigation mandatory)
- Failure to respond to oral iron
- Recurrent IDA without identified cause
- Haematology referral: suspected haematological malignancy, concomitant B12/folate deficiency
